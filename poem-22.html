The Roommate

By Vala Newman

I’ve struggled to be happy
my whole life.
I don’t know why—
it’s just always been this way.

Lately,
I’m no longer quietly not okay.
Now the sadness shows its teeth.

When I’m sad,
I’m told I want attention.
When I don’t speak,
my silence becomes permission for excuses.

I am taught rules for grief:
I can’t be sad because others have it worse.
I can’t be sad because I never said anything.
I can’t be sad because my sadness is inconvenient.

But if you were paying attention,
you would’ve seen the child
sent to her room like a princess in a tower—
not protected,
just contained.

Then my mother got sick,
and the tower disappeared.

So I hid elsewhere.
In books.
In being useful.
In making myself smaller
so I could be loved without complaint.

I learned to apologize
for existing too loudly,
too honestly,
too much.

Growing up,
I learned how to hurt myself quietly.
Not with blades—
with bruises I could blame on sleep.
With hands against my own face,
just to feel something other than this
endless, echoing ache.

People I loved passed on,
the ones who made life feel survivable.
And somehow,
I stayed.

I traded pain for substances,
weed, alcohol, mushrooms—
because happiness found me there.
Laughter without permission.
A brighter world, briefly believable.

But the world is dark.
People get sad.
People learn to cope.

And now—
I’ve stopped hiding.
No substances.
No locked rooms.
No violence turned inward.

Just me,
finally sitting with the sadness.

I’ve lived through more than what is considered normal.
Most people would have shattered.
I bent.

I am not perfect.
But I am strong enough
to live with my sadness
like a roommate—
the twin I never asked for,
never wanted,
but learned to love anyway.
